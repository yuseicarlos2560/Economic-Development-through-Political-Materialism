\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{The measurement problem in comparative political economy}

Recent economic models attempt to capture latent political variables such as democracy metrics. Some of the most widely used metrics in academia today are V-Dem and Polity-V; however, \textcite{treier_jackman_2008} showed significant error in Polity-V's democracy measures that lead to significant inference errors. In another study, \textcite{little_meng_2024} challenged recent claims of democratic backsliding in recent years and found that such claims are not supported by objective data such as election results, competitiveness of elections, and journalists jailed and murdered; their findings suggest that it is the experts' biases that are changing instead of the objective measures.

These measurement problems matter beyond the democracy literature. If the standard indicators of a country's political character are partly artifacts of expert judgment, then any theory of economic development built on those indicators inherits their errors. This is the entry point of the present work.

\subsection{The institutional theory of development and its limits}

One of the most influential works in recent years has been that of Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson (hereafter AJR), who developed a theory of Inclusive and Extractive Institutions that attempts to explain why two territories with similar material conditions can develop very differently, as in the case of Nogales, Arizona, and Nogales, Sonora \parencite{acemoglu_johnson_robinson_2001,acemoglu_johnson_robinson_2005,acemoglu_robinson_2012}. The main drawback of this theory is its inability to develop a theory of the state; instead, it relies on economic indicators and experts' opinions to classify a nation as Inclusive (II) or Extractive (EI). There are, however, at least two flaws in this model. \textit{I) Circular measurement.} Inclusive Institutions are defined by the outcomes they are meant to explain, such as a low expropriation rate and secure property rights. Claiming that a country with Inclusive Institutions displays a low expropriation rate ($A \to X$) licenses the reverse inference that a country displaying a low expropriation rate must have Inclusive Institutions ($X \to A$); the classification then cannot independently explain the outcome, because the outcome was used to construct the classification. \textit{II) Vague horizon.} AJR attempt to answer this objection by arguing that some Extractive Institutions can sustain inclusive economic policies, but that such economies are destined to fail in the long term. Because no time bound is specified, this is an unfalsifiable claim: any continuing success can be reclassified as ``not yet long term.''

A related strand of research links democratization to growth directly. \textcite{acemoglu_naidu_restrepo_robinson_2019} find that transitions to democracy cause subsequent economic growth. Yet this study, too, relies on democracy indicators rather than on a direct analysis of state structures, and the findings of \textcite{little_meng_2024} cast doubt on whether the underlying correlations reflect objective changes in these economies or changes in the biases of the experts who rate them. What is needed, therefore, is a theoretical framework that describes domestic politics and international position without depending on contested expert-coded indices.

\subsection{Research question and hypotheses}

This work asks: \emph{which changes in a country's political and international situation actually produce major economic discontinuities---sharp breaks in a country's economic trajectory, whether downward or upward?} To answer it, we must first separate events that the literature habitually lumps together under ``regime change'': the replacement of officeholders, the replacement of the coalition sustaining them, changes in the formal rules of access to power, transformations of the effective structure of political power, restructuring of the state apparatus, policy change, and shifts in a state's external alignment. Section~\ref{sec:framework} defines each of these terms in detail.

The following hypotheses build on these concepts and turn the research question into empirically testable claims.

\begin{itemize}
    \item $H_1$ on government turnover: For any two countries $A$ and $B$ with comparable material endowments and similar external alignment, government turnover---and even nominal regime change---that leaves the effective structure of political power untouched will not produce a major economic discontinuity.
    \item $H_2$ on external realignment: For any two such countries, variation in major economic discontinuities tracks variation in external alignment rather than variation in domestic political events. The sign of the discontinuity depends on the type of realignment: severance from a dominant power's sphere of influence deprives the state of material capabilities and produces \emph{disruption}, whereas entry into a richer sphere supplies material capabilities and produces \emph{acceleration}.
    \item $H_3$ on the interaction of domestic and international change: A country that undergoes domestic political change will experience a major economic discontinuity only when that change coincides with an external realignment; and external realignment produces a major discontinuity---disruption or acceleration, according to its type---whether or not domestic political change accompanies it.
\end{itemize}

Note that $H_3$ does not follow logically from $H_1$ and $H_2$; it is a stronger claim and must be tested separately. Section~\ref{sec:hypotheses} derives the observable implications of the three hypotheses in a comparative design.

\section{Theoretical Framework}
\label{sec:framework}

This framework is organized around five questions: \textbf{who governs}, \textbf{how political power is organized}, \textbf{through which permanent institutions power is exercised}, \textbf{where the state sits internationally}, and \textbf{what outcomes follow}.

\subsection{Units of analysis}

\begin{itemize}
	\item A \emph{country} is the territorial, geographical, demographic, or economic unit; the term is used when comparing geographical conditions or economic performance, not when analyzing the state as a political organization \parencite{linz_stepan_1996,waltz_1979}.
	\item The \emph{state} is used in two related senses: domestically, the durable political organization possessing administrative and coercive authority over a territory \parencite{weber1922wirtschaft,hobbes1651leviathan}; internationally, the corporate actor whose alignment and position change relative to other states \parencite{waltz_1979,mearsheimer_2001}. The state is neither the sum of all publicly funded bodies nor the incumbent government.
	\item The \emph{international system} is the wider structure within which states operate, characterized by anarchy and by the distribution of capabilities, especially among great powers; the structure constrains behavior but is not itself an actor \parencite{waltz_1979,mearsheimer_2001}. Where cases sit inside a particular regional distribution of power, the relevant unit is the \emph{regional subsystem} \parencite{buzan_waever_2003}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Domestic political elements and actors}

\begin{itemize}
	\item The \emph{government} (or incumbent administration) denotes the persons and organizations currently holding governing office: presidents, prime ministers, ministers, and, where relevant, the ruling coalition. It answers ``who governs?''. Policies and international behavior are things the government \emph{does}; they are not part of what the government \emph{is} \parencite{linz_stepan_1996}.
	\item The \emph{ruling coalition} is the wider set of parties, elites, and organized groups that sustain the government in power; replacing a president or governing party does not necessarily replace it \parencite{bueno_de_mesquita_2003}.
	\item The \emph{political regime} comprises the rules and institutional arrangements governing access to political power, the distribution of authority among institutions, competition for office, and control or accountability over rulers; it answers ``how is political power formally organized?'' \parencite{linz_stepan_1996,linz_2000}.
	\item The \emph{structure of political power}, by contrast, captures the \emph{effective} organization and control of power: the actual separation or concentration of powers, the independence of representation, the party--state relationship, and the capacity of citizens or rival elites to control rulers \parencite{garciatrevijano2010}. A country may change its formal regime while its structure of political power remains intact; Garc{\'i}a-Trevijano's analysis of the party-state (\emph{Estado de partidos}) describes exactly this case: state-integrated parties collectively control political power behind formally democratic institutions \parencite{garciatrevijano2010}.
	\item \emph{Political parties} are organizations that may participate in government, opposition, or the ruling coalition, and are not automatically part of the government \parencite{sartori_1976}.
	\item The \emph{state apparatus} is the durable administrative, coercive, judicial, and fiscal machinery through which political authority is exercised \parencite{weber1922wirtschaft,althusser_1971}. It has three main components:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item the \emph{coercive apparatus} (armed forces, police, security services; \textcite{tilly_1990,althusser_1971}),
		\item the \emph{administrative apparatus} (ministries and public bureaucracy; \textcite{weber1922wirtschaft}), and
		\item the \emph{judicial apparatus} (courts and related institutions; \textcite{weber1922wirtschaft,garciatrevijano2010}).
	\end{itemize}
	\item The \emph{public sector} is a residual category for publicly owned or funded bodies such as hospitals, schools, and state companies. Being part of the public sector does not automatically make an organization part of the core state apparatus, nor a strategic political actor.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{International actors and structural elements}

\begin{itemize}
	\item A \emph{great power} is a state with military capabilities large enough to play a major role in the distribution of power \parencite{mearsheimer_2001}.
	\item In asymmetric relationships, the \emph{dominant power} is the stronger state and the \emph{subordinate state} the one whose security, alignment, or foreign-policy choices are substantially constrained by it; international relations frequently exhibit such hierarchy rather than formal equivalence among states \parencite{lake_2009}. 
	\item A \emph{hegemon} is a state powerful enough to dominate the others in the relevant system or region \parencite{mearsheimer_2001}. An \emph{aligned state} only coordinates its external orientation with another power; alignment alone does not imply subordination \parencite{walt_1987}.
	\item A \emph{sphere of influence} is a determinate region or relationship in which an external power exercises predominant influence, limiting the independence of the political entities within it, without necessarily implying complete control \parencite{keal_1983,hast_2014}.
	\item A state's \emph{external alignment} is its position vis-\`a-vis great powers, alliances, and spheres of influence \parencite{walt_1987}; its \emph{position within the international structure} is its location relative to other powers, so that a change of alignment is a positional change and does not by itself constitute a change in the structure \parencite{waltz_1979}.
\end{itemize}



\subsection{What actually changes: an event taxonomy}

The literature often uses ``regime change'' for several different events at once. This framework separates them into the following categories.

\begin{itemize}
	\item \emph{Government turnover} replaces the persons or parties holding office \parencite{bueno_de_mesquita_2003}.
	\item \emph{Ruling-coalition change} replaces the wider set of actors whose support sustains rule \parencite{bueno_de_mesquita_2003}.
	\item \emph{Regime change} alters the formal rules governing access to and organization of political power \parencite{linz_stepan_1996}. 
	\item A \emph{transformation of the power structure} alters the effective concentration, separation, representation, or control of political power, making it possible to distinguish nominal regime change from deeper structural continuity \parencite{garciatrevijano2010}.
	\item \emph{State-apparatus restructuring} transforms the durable administrative, coercive, judicial, or fiscal machinery of the state \parencite{skocpol_1979,althusser_1971}. 
	\item \emph{Policy change} alters what the government does---its settings, instruments, or overarching goals---and is neither turnover nor regime change \parencite{hall_1993}.
	\item An \emph{external realignment} changes a state's alliance, dominant-power relationship, or sphere of influence \parencite{walt_1987}. A \emph{systemic power shift}, in contrast, changes the overall distribution of capabilities among states \parencite{gilpin_1981,waltz_1979}.
\end{itemize}

Two distinctions matter most here: government turnover, and even nominal regime change, may occur without a transformation of the underlying structure of political power; and a state's external realignment may occur without a systemic power shift, although the two may coincide.

\subsection{Outcomes and conditioning conditions}

The dependent phenomenon is a \emph{major economic discontinuity}: a sharp and sustained break in a country's prior economic trajectory, in either direction. Its negative form, \emph{economic disruption}, covers output collapse, inflation, or capital flight \parencite{rodrik_1999}; its positive form, \emph{economic acceleration}, covers sustained take-off and convergence with richer economies. Which of the two follows an external realignment depends on whether the realignment deprives the state of material capabilities or supplies them---that is, on whether the benefits of a dominant-power relationship (market access, capital, security guarantees) are withdrawn or gained \parencite{lake_2009,gilpin_1981}. Finally, \emph{material endowments} (geography, natural resources, environment, population) are relatively durable conditions that constrain economic and strategic possibilities \parencite{diamond_1997}, while \emph{material capabilities} are the resources a state can actually mobilize---endowments are conditions, capabilities are politically usable capacities \parencite{mearsheimer_2001}.

In synthesis, states are governed by incumbent governments, supported by ruling coalitions, operating within a political regime and an effective structure of political power, and acting through a durable state apparatus; internationally, states possess an external alignment and a position within the international structure, especially in relation to great powers and their spheres of influence; and their economic and military outcomes are conditioned by their material endowments and capabilities.

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:lit_review}

The research question draws on four bodies of literature.

\paragraph{Institutions and development.} AJR's institutional theory \parencite{acemoglu_johnson_robinson_2001,acemoglu_johnson_robinson_2005,acemoglu_robinson_2012} and the democratization--growth literature \parencite{acemoglu_naidu_restrepo_robinson_2019} explain development through the character of domestic institutions, but measure that character through expert-coded indices whose reliability is contested \parencite{treier_jackman_2008,little_meng_2024}, and---as argued in Section~\ref{sec:background}---through classifications that risk circularity and unfalsifiability. Neither strand distinguishes the replacement of rulers from the transformation of the power structure; both therefore over-attribute economic outcomes to visible domestic political change.

\paragraph{Regimes, power structures, and the state.} The comparative-politics literature supplies the missing domestic distinctions. \textcite{linz_stepan_1996} and \textcite{linz_2000} separate state, regime, and government; \textcite{garciatrevijano2010} adds the effective structure of political power and the party-state paradigm; \textcite{sartori_1976} and \textcite{bueno_de_mesquita_2003} clarify parties and support coalitions; and \textcite{weber1922wirtschaft}, \textcite{althusser_1971}, \textcite{tilly_1990}, and \textcite{skocpol_1979} theorize the durable state apparatus that persists through changes of rulers. \textcite{hall_1993} shows that even policy change comes in distinct orders, so that not every observable policy shift signals deep political change.

\paragraph{International structure and hierarchy.} Structural realism treats states as like units positioned within an anarchic system whose distribution of capabilities constrains them \parencite{waltz_1979}, with great powers competing for power and regional hegemony \parencite{mearsheimer_2001}. Alignment and alliance choices \parencite{walt_1987}, hierarchy between dominant and subordinate states \parencite{lake_2009}, spheres of influence \parencite{keal_1983,hast_2014}, regional security complexes \parencite{buzan_waever_2003}, and systemic power shifts \parencite{gilpin_1981} supply the international half of the framework.

\paragraph{Material conditions and shocks.} Geographical and environmental endowments condition development possibilities \parencite{diamond_1997}, and population and wealth convert into latent and military power \parencite{mearsheimer_2001}. \textcite{rodrik_1999} shows that growth collapses following external shocks are mediated by domestic conflict-management institutions---evidence consistent with the present claim that external position, not domestic political labels, drives disruption.

The gap this thesis occupies is the intersection of these streams: no existing framework explains major economic discontinuities by jointly separating (i) domestic events that change rulers from those that change power structures, and (ii) a state's positional realignment from systemic power shifts. The hypotheses of Section~\ref{sec:background} state exactly this joint claim.

\section{Hypotheses and Empirical Implications}
\label{sec:hypotheses}

The three hypotheses translate into the comparative design of Table~\ref{tab:design}. Cases are selected as pairs of countries with comparable material endowments; the design then crosses (rows) the type of domestic political change with (columns) the type of external realignment: none, severance from a dominant power's sphere, or entry into a richer sphere.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\begin{threeparttable}
\caption{Predicted economic discontinuities by type of political and international change}
\label{tab:design}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.26\linewidth}p{0.20\linewidth}p{0.22\linewidth}p{0.22\linewidth}@{}}
\toprule
 & No external realignment & Severance from a sphere & Entry into a richer sphere \\
\midrule
Government turnover / nominal regime change only & No major discontinuity ($H_1$) & Disruption ($H_2$, $H_3$) & Acceleration ($H_2$, $H_3$) \\
Power-structure transformation or apparatus restructuring & Ambiguous; out of scope of $H_1$ & Disruption ($H_2$) & Acceleration ($H_2$) \\
No domestic political change & No major discontinuity & Disruption ($H_2$, $H_3$) & Acceleration ($H_2$, $H_3$) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\begin{tablenotes}
\small
\item Note: Predictions assume comparable material endowments across compared cases. \emph{Severance} means the loss of a dominant-power relationship's material benefits (market access, capital, security guarantees); \emph{entry} means gaining them \parencite{walt_1987,keal_1983,lake_2009}. Domestic event categories follow Section~\ref{sec:framework}.
\end{tablenotes}
\end{threeparttable}
\end{table}

Three features make these hypotheses falsifiable. First, the event categories are defined independently of the outcome, so cases can be classified without circularity. Second, $H_1$ fails if matched cases show a major discontinuity following turnover that left the power structure and external alignment untouched. Third, $H_2$ and $H_3$ fail if major discontinuities appear without realignment, if realignment produces no discontinuity, or if the sign is wrong---acceleration after severance, or disruption after entry into a richer sphere. It would be tempting to state $H_3$ as a biconditional---discontinuity occurs if and only if domestic change coincides with external realignment---but that stronger claim does not follow from $H_1$ and $H_2$. $H_3$ is therefore advanced as a separate empirical prediction, to be tested on matched pairs of countries. Two coding rules discipline the design: outcomes must follow, not precede, the realignment they are attributed to; and the threshold for acceleration is set symmetrically to that for disruption.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

The empirical strategy has two layers: comparative case analysis, which carries the causal narrative, and statistical analysis, which summarizes the same predictions over a larger number of observations. Both layers test the same hypotheses with the same coding rules, so that a failure in either layer counts against the theory.

\subsection{Comparative case analysis}

Cases are selected as matched pairs of countries with comparable material endowments (Section~\ref{sec:hypotheses}) and coded according to the event taxonomy of Section~\ref{sec:framework} and the outcome definition of Section~\ref{sec:framework}: a \emph{disruption} threshold in the spirit of \textcite{rodrik_1999} and a symmetric \emph{acceleration} threshold in the spirit of \textcite{hausmann_pritchett_rodrik_2005}, both fixed before the cases are coded. Within each case, process tracing establishes the sequence of events, and the timing rule of Section~\ref{sec:hypotheses} is enforced: an outcome is attributed to a realignment only if it follows it. The comparative design and its falsification criteria are those of Table~\ref{tab:design}.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}

The statistical layer proceeds in two steps. The first step is descriptive and involves no model: country-years are classified into the cells of Table~\ref{tab:design}, and we report the frequency of major economic discontinuities per cell together with mean growth following each type of realignment. If the theory is correct, discontinuities should concentrate in the severance and entry columns, with opposite signs, while the no-realignment column remains quiet regardless of domestic political events.

The second step is a linear regression on a country-year panel, extending the specification of \textcite{acemoglu_naidu_restrepo_robinson_2019}: we keep their skeleton---real GDP per capita on country fixed effects, year fixed effects, and lags of the political variable---but replace their expert-coded democracy index with event variables coded from observable facts:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
g_{it} = \alpha_i + \lambda_t &+ \beta_1 \mathrm{Severance}_{it} + \beta_2 \mathrm{Entry}_{it} + \beta_3 \mathrm{Turnover}_{it} \\
&+ \beta_4 \mathrm{RegimeChange}_{it} + \beta_5 \mathrm{PowerStructure}_{it} + \gamma' X_{it} + \varepsilon_{it},
\end{split}
\label{eq:main}
\end{equation}
where $g_{it}$ is real GDP per capita growth in country $i$ in year $t$; $\alpha_i$ and $\lambda_t$ are country and year fixed effects; the event indicators equal one in the year of the event and are also included with lags, so that effects can materialize over several years \parencite{acemoglu_naidu_restrepo_robinson_2019}; and $X_{it}$ collects controls for the material channel (oil rents as a share of GDP, population, investment share). Standard errors are clustered by country.

The hypotheses translate into restrictions on the coefficients. $H_1$ predicts $\beta_3 = \beta_4 = 0$: turnover and nominal regime change alone have no effect. $H_2$ predicts $\beta_1 < 0$ (severance produces disruption) and $\beta_2 > 0$ (entry into a richer sphere produces acceleration). $H_3$ is examined by interacting the domestic event indicators with the realignment indicators. The coefficient on power-structure transformation, $\beta_5$, is left free, since the theory makes no claim about that row of Table~\ref{tab:design}.

The sample is restricted to the regions covered by the case survey---Latin America, the post-Soviet space, East Asia, and Europe---over 1960--2019; extending the panel to a global sample is left for later work. Robustness checks include alternative outcome thresholds, the exclusion of the systemic-shock years 1989--1991, and synthetic-control estimates for the two most extreme cases, Venezuela and Cuba \parencite{abadie_diamond_hainmueller_2010}.

\subsection{Coding rules}
\label{sec:coding}

All variables are coded from observable events according to rules fixed in advance; the full protocol is documented in the replication materials. Three rule sets carry the design.

\paragraph{Outcome thresholds.} A \emph{disruption} is coded when real GDP per capita contracts by at least 15 percent peak-to-trough within five years, or growth falls at least three percentage points below its prior five-year average for at least three years, or annual inflation exceeds 500 percent \parencite{rodrik_1999}. An \emph{acceleration} is coded when growth of at least 3.5 percent per year is sustained for at least eight years, at least two percentage points above the prior growth regime \parencite{hausmann_pritchett_rodrik_2005}. The thresholds are symmetric by construction and are fixed before any case is coded.

\paragraph{Domestic political events.} Turnover is coded from leader records \parencite{goemans_gleditsch_chiozza_2009}; regime change from constitutional events \parencite{elkins_ginsburg_melton_2009}. A \emph{transformation of the power structure}---the category that separates nominal regime change from deep structural change---is coded only when at least two of four dimensions change within a three-year window, each anchored to a primary legal or institutional act: the separation or concentration of powers in practice, the independence of representation, the fusion of ruling party and state, and the capacity to control rulers \parencite{garciatrevijano2010}.

\paragraph{External realignment.} Following \textcite{keal_1983}, a sphere of influence requires predominant influence, not formal control; membership is coded when influence is documented in at least two of four indicator families: security ties \parencite{leeds_etal_2002,gibler_sarkees_2004}, economic dependence \parencite{imf_dots}, institutional bloc membership, and coercion \parencite{felbermayr_etal_2020}. \emph{Severance} is the termination of at least two such ties within a three-year window; \emph{entry} is their establishment. Voluntariness is coded by patron continuity: a severance caused by the patron's collapse or withdrawal is \emph{imposed}, and these episodes carry the causal weight of the design. A sphere counts as \emph{richer} when the patron's GDP per capita exceeds the regional median in the entry year, which makes the sign prediction of $H_2$ mechanical rather than judgmental. Gradual severances are interval-coded, with robustness at both endpoints. All codings are validated against UN General Assembly voting records \parencite{bailey_strezhnev_voeten_2017}: a severance should coincide with voting divergence from the patron, an entry with convergence.

\paragraph{Reliability.} A random subset of episodes is double-coded, targeting Cohen's $\kappa \geq 0.7$; all disagreements and their resolutions are published with the replication files.

With these rules, every cell of Table~\ref{tab:design} and every coefficient restriction of equation~\eqref{eq:main} is generated by a published, replicable procedure rather than by case-by-case judgment.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

The analysis combines six groups of sources, all public.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Economic outcomes.} Real GDP per capita and its growth, inflation, and capital flows come from the World Bank's World Development Indicators \parencite{worldbank_wdi} and the IMF's World Economic Outlook \parencite{imf_weo}, with two coverage caveats: the IMF does not monitor Cuba or North Korea, which are not member countries, so Cuban outcomes rely on ECLAC's CEPALSTAT database \parencite{eclac_cepalstat} and national sources; and WEO figures for Venezuela are staff estimates, since no Article IV consultation has taken place since 2004. Long-run levels come from the Maddison Project Database \parencite{bolt_vanzanden_2024}, which covers all case countries; its series for the post-Soviet republics begin in 1990, with earlier years available only for the USSR as a whole.
    \item \emph{Domestic political events.} Government turnover and its manner (elections, coups, successions) are coded from the Archigos database of political leaders \parencite{goemans_gleditsch_chiozza_2009}, which records every effective leader's entry into and exit from office for 1875--2015; turnover after 2015 is coded by hand from constitutional records. Regime changes and transformations of the power structure are coded from primary constitutional and legal documents, using the Comparative Constitutions Project as the reference catalogue of constitutional events \parencite{elkins_ginsburg_melton_2009}.
    \item \emph{International alignment.} Alliance membership and its changes are coded from the ATOP dataset, which covers all formal alliance treaties in 1815--2018 \parencite{leeds_etal_2002}, and the Correlates of War formal alliance dataset, covering 1816--2012 \parencite{gibler_sarkees_2004}, supplemented by the historical record of patron--client relationships (for example, the Soviet--Cuban and Venezuelan--Cuban subsidy agreements).
    \item \emph{Material conditions and controls.} Geography, population, natural-resource rents (oil, gas, and minerals as shares of GDP), and investment come from the World Development Indicators \parencite{worldbank_wdi}.
    \item \emph{Regional sources.} EBRD \emph{Transition Reports}, published annually since 1994 \parencite{ebrd_transition}, for the post-Soviet cluster; national statistical offices and central banks for Venezuela (BCV) and Cuba (ONEI), used with documented caveats where official reporting is interrupted.
    \item \emph{Deliberately excluded.} Expert-coded indices (V-Dem, Polity) are not used as explanatory variables, given the measurement problems discussed in Section~\ref{sec:background}; they appear only as objects of comparison.
\end{itemize}

The panel is unbalanced: coverage for the post-Soviet republics begins in 1990--1991, alliance data end in 2012 (COW) or 2018 (ATOP), and some series for Venezuela and Cuba contain gaps; all such gaps will be documented in the replication materials. All coding decisions and the full dataset will be published with the thesis.

\section{Limitations and Conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Four limitations of this research design should be acknowledged. First, \emph{major economic discontinuity} still requires operationalization: a disruption threshold along the lines suggested by \textcite{rodrik_1999} and a symmetric acceleration threshold along the lines of \textcite{hausmann_pritchett_rodrik_2005}. Second, the boundary between nominal regime change and genuine power-structure transformation is analytically clear \parencite{garciatrevijano2010,linz_stepan_1996} but empirically demanding to code; historical cases of state-apparatus restructuring \parencite{skocpol_1979} will guide the coding rules. Third, the framework is deliberately agnostic about normative evaluations of regimes: it classifies how power is organized, not whether that organization is desirable. Fourth, entry into a richer sphere is partly endogenous---patrons often admit states that are already reforming---so the severance cases, in which realignment was imposed rather than chosen, must carry the causal weight of the design.

The central claim of the thesis can be stated simply. Much of what is usually attributed to ``regime change'' is, on this account, the effect of external realignment: a state changing its position within the international structure. When realignment severs a dominant-power relationship, the result is disruption; when it anchors the state in a richer sphere, the result is acceleration. Domestic political change that leaves the structure of political power untouched, meanwhile, leaves the economy on its prior path. The empirical chapters will test these claims on matched pairs of countries.
