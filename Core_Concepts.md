# Core Concepts

## How to read the source notes

- **Main author/source** identifies the principal theoretical anchor used here; it does not necessarily mean that the author coined the term.
- **Main related work(s)** gives the most relevant chapters, sections, or pages. Chapter titles follow the editions listed in the bibliography.
- Some entries are deliberately **framework conventions** or generic analytical labels. Assigning them a single originator would be misleading.
- References to García-Trevijano use the 2010 edition of *Teoría pura de la república*. Page references are given where a reliable chapter or section title could not be verified.

## Guiding distinction

The conceptual apparatus should not be organized around **“regime–establishment–Leviathan.”** That triad mixes officeholders, institutions, policies, and the state as a whole. Instead, separate:

1. **Who governs**
2. **How political power is organized**
3. **Through which permanent institutions power is exercised**
4. **Where the state sits internationally**
5. **What outcomes follow**

## 1. Units of analysis

### Country

**Definition.** The territorial, geographical, demographic, or economic unit. Use *country* when comparing geographical conditions or economic performance, rather than when analyzing the state as a political organization or international actor.

**Main author/source.** No single originator; this is a descriptive unit label and a convention of the present framework.

**Main related work(s).** Linz and Stepan, *Problems of Democratic Transition and Consolidation* (1996), Chapter 2, **“‘Stateness,’ Nationalism, and Democratization”**, for the distinction between a territorial-political unit and the condition of stateness. Waltz, *Theory of International Politics* (1979), Chapter 5, **“Political Structures”**, for the analytical distinction between units and the structure in which they are located.

### State

**Definition.** Used in two related senses:

- **Domestically:** the durable political organization possessing administrative and coercive authority over a territory.
- **Internationally:** the corporate actor whose alignment and position change relative to other states.

The state is not simply the sum of all publicly funded bodies, nor should it be equated with the incumbent government.

**Main author/source.** **Max Weber** for the domestic political organization; **Kenneth N. Waltz** and **John J. Mearsheimer** for the state as an international actor.

**Main related work(s).** Weber, *Economy and Society* (1978 [1922]), Chapter 1, **“Basic Sociological Terms,” §17, “Political and Hierocratic Organizations”**, for the political organization, territory, administration, and legitimate physical force. Waltz, *Theory of International Politics* (1979), Chapters 5–6, **“Political Structures”** and **“Anarchic Orders and Balances of Power”**, for states as like units in an anarchic system. Mearsheimer, *The Tragedy of Great Power Politics* (2001), Chapter 2, **“Anarchy and the Struggle for Power”**, for states as the principal actors in offensive realism.

### International system

**Definition.** The wider structure within which states operate. Its relevant features include **anarchy** and the **distribution of capabilities or power**, especially among great powers. The structure constrains behavior; it is not itself an actor.

**Main author/source.** **Kenneth N. Waltz** for systemic structure; **John J. Mearsheimer** for its offensive-realist implications.

**Main related work(s).** Waltz, *Theory of International Politics* (1979), Chapter 5, **“Political Structures”**, for ordering principle, differentiation of units, and distribution of capabilities; Chapter 6, **“Anarchic Orders and Balances of Power”**, for anarchy and self-help. Mearsheimer, *The Tragedy of Great Power Politics* (2001), Chapter 2, **“Anarchy and the Struggle for Power”**, especially its five assumptions about the international system.

### Regional subsystem

**Definition.** The relevant regional structure within the wider international system. Use this term when the cases are situated primarily within a particular regional distribution of power and security relationships rather than the global system as a whole.

**Main author/source.** **Barry Buzan and Ole Wæver**, within regional security complex theory. *Regional subsystem* is used here as a general analytical label, not as a claim that Buzan and Wæver coined this exact phrase.

**Main related work(s).** Buzan and Wæver, *Regions and Powers: The Structure of International Security* (2003), **Part I, “Theoretical Framework”**, for the regional level, regional security complexes, and their relationship to global-level powers.

## 2. Domestic political elements and actors

### Government / incumbent administration

**Definition.** The persons and organizations currently holding governing office: presidents, prime ministers, ministers, and—where relevant—the ruling coalition. This answers **“Who governs?”** Policies and international behavior are the government’s actions, not part of the definition of the actor itself.

**Main author/source.** No single originator; this is standard comparative-political usage. **Juan J. Linz and Alfred Stepan** provide the closest source for maintaining the analytical distinction between government, regime, and state.

**Main related work(s).** Linz and Stepan, *Problems of Democratic Transition and Consolidation* (1996), Chapter 1, **“Democracy and Its Arenas”**, especially the discussion of political society, rule of law, and the usable state bureaucracy. García-Trevijano, *Teoría pura de la república* (2010), **p. 565**, for his more specific institutional distinction between executive government, legislative power, and judicial power; this should not be treated as a general definition of incumbent administration.

### Ruling coalition

**Definition.** The set of parties, elites, and other organized groups that sustain the government in power. This is useful when replacing a president or governing party does not necessarily replace the wider coalition controlling political power.

**Main author/source.** No single originator for the broad term. **Bruce Bueno de Mesquita, Alastair Smith, Randolph M. Siverson, and James D. Morrow** supply the most relevant formal account of the support coalition needed for political survival, although their narrower technical term is **winning coalition**, not simply ruling coalition.

**Main related work(s).** Bueno de Mesquita et al., *The Logic of Political Survival* (2003), Chapter 1, **“The Logic of Political Survival”**, and Chapter 2, **“The Selectorate Theory”**, for selectorate, winning coalition, leadership support, and replacement.

### Political regime

**Definition.** The rules and institutional arrangements governing access to political power, the distribution of authority among institutions, competition for office, and control or accountability over rulers. It answers **“How is political power formally organized and exercised?”**

**Main author/source.** **Juan J. Linz** for regime classification; **Juan J. Linz and Alfred Stepan** for the distinction among regime, state, and the arenas of democratic politics.

**Main related work(s).** Linz and Stepan, *Problems of Democratic Transition and Consolidation* (1996), Chapter 1, **“Democracy and Its Arenas”**, and Chapter 3, **“Modern Nondemocratic Regimes”**. Linz, *Totalitarian and Authoritarian Regimes* (2000), for the broader typology of political regimes.

### Structure of political power

**Definition.** The **effective organization and control of power**, rather than the country’s official classification as democratic, monarchical, republican, or authoritarian. It directs attention to the separation or concentration of powers, the independence of representation, the party–state relationship, and the capacity of citizens or rival elites to control rulers.

In this framework, **political regime** describes formal-institutional arrangements, while **structure of political power** captures their effective operation.

**Main author/source.** **Antonio García-Trevijano**. The English label is adopted here as an analytical rendering of his concern with the *estructura del poder político en el Estado*; it should not be presented as a standardized English technical term used across the literature.

**Main related work(s).** García-Trevijano, *Teoría pura de la república* (2010), **p. 81**, for the structure of political power in the state; **p. 248**, for the four paradigms of political organization; **pp. 369 and 374**, for control of rulers, political freedom, representation, and separation of powers.

### State apparatus

**Definition.** The durable administrative, coercive, judicial, and fiscal organizations through which political authority is exercised. It replaces **establishment**, which is too broad when defined by state funding.

The term is used here institutionally; adopting it does not require adopting Althusser’s complete theory of class reproduction or ideological state apparatuses.

**Main author/source.** **Max Weber** for administration and domination; **Louis Althusser** for the explicit distinction between state power and state apparatus.

**Main related work(s).** Weber, *Economy and Society* (1978 [1922]), Chapter 1, **§17, “Political and Hierocratic Organizations”**, and Chapter 11, **“Bureaucracy”**. Althusser, **“Ideology and Ideological State Apparatuses (Notes towards an Investigation)”** (1971), sections **“The State”** and **“The State Ideological Apparatuses”**, especially the distinction between state power, the repressive state apparatus, and ideological apparatuses.

#### Coercive apparatus

**Definition.** The armed forces, police, security services, and related organizations specializing in coercion.

**Main author/source.** **Charles Tilly** for the historical relationship among coercion, war, extraction, and state formation; **Louis Althusser** for the narrower category of the repressive state apparatus.

**Main related work(s).** Tilly, *Coercion, Capital, and European States, AD 990–1990* (1990), Chapter 1, **“Cities and States in World History”**, and Chapter 3, **“How War Made States, and Vice Versa”**. Althusser, **“Ideology and Ideological State Apparatuses”**, section **“The State”**.

#### Administrative apparatus

**Definition.** Ministries, public bureaucracy, and the organizations responsible for routine administration and implementation.

**Main author/source.** **Max Weber**.

**Main related work(s).** Weber, *Economy and Society* (1978 [1922]), Chapter 11, **“Bureaucracy”**, for bureaucratic organization, official duties, hierarchy, and administration.

#### Judicial apparatus

**Definition.** Courts and related institutions through which legal disputes are adjudicated and legal authority is applied.

**Main author/source.** No single originator for this descriptive subtype. **Max Weber** is the principal related source for legal domination and rational legal institutions; **García-Trevijano** is relevant specifically to judicial independence within his proposed republican order.

**Main related work(s).** Weber, *Economy and Society* (1978 [1922]), Chapter 3, **“The Types of Legitimate Domination”**, for legal authority. García-Trevijano, *Teoría pura de la república* (2010), **p. 565**, for judicial legitimacy, independence, and the coordination of separated powers.

#### Public sector

**Definition.** A residual descriptive category covering publicly owned or publicly funded bodies, including hospitals, schools, and state companies. Membership in the public sector does not automatically make an organization part of the core state apparatus or a strategic political actor.

**Main author/source.** No single originator; this is a generic administrative and public-finance category, not a concept borrowed from Mearsheimer or García-Trevijano.

**Main related work(s).** No specific theoretical chapter is assigned here. If public ownership, taxation, or expenditure becomes analytically important, a separate public-finance source should be introduced rather than treating *public sector* as part of realist or Trevijano’s terminology.

### Political parties

**Definition.** Organizations that may participate in government, opposition, or the ruling coalition. Parties should not automatically be included in the definition of government.

**Main author/source.** **Giovanni Sartori** for parties and party systems in comparative politics.

**Main related work(s).** Sartori, *Parties and Party Systems: A Framework for Analysis*, Volume 1 (1976), Chapter 1, **“The Party as Part”**, for the conceptualization of parties and their relationship to the wider political system.

### State parties / party-state oligarchy

**Definition.** Theoretically specific terms for parties integrated into and sustained by the state, and for a political order in which such parties collectively control political power. They are not general synonyms for political parties.

**Main author/source.** **Antonio García-Trevijano**, particularly his concepts of *Estado de partidos*, *partidos estatales*, and *oligarquía de partidos*. **Party-state oligarchy** is an English composite used here, not a claim about his one preferred English translation.

**Main related work(s).** García-Trevijano, *Teoría pura de la república* (2010), **p. 248**, for the paradigm of the party-state; **pp. 349 and 369**, for state parties, oligarchy, and the absence of political control; **p. 468**, for proportional representation, state parties, and the relationship between electoral and civil orders.

## 3. International actors and structural elements

### Great power

**Definition.** A state possessing sufficiently large military capabilities to play a major role in the distribution of power and security competition. Use this term rather than the looser *major power* when working specifically within Mearsheimer’s vocabulary.

**Main author/source.** **John J. Mearsheimer**, building on the wider realist tradition.

**Main related work(s).** Mearsheimer, *The Tragedy of Great Power Politics* (2001), Chapter 2, **“Anarchy and the Struggle for Power”**, for great-power behavior and hegemony; Chapter 3, **“Wealth and Power”**, for latent power; Chapter 4, **“The Primacy of Land Power”**, for military power.

### Dominant power

**Definition.** The stronger state in an asymmetric international relationship, particularly within a sphere of influence or hierarchy.

**Main author/source.** No single originator for the generic label. **David A. Lake** is the principal related source for analyzing international relations as hierarchical rather than assuming that all interstate relationships are formally equivalent.

**Main related work(s).** Lake, *Hierarchy in International Relations* (2009), Chapter 1, **“International Hierarchy”**, and Chapter 2, **“Relational Contracting and International Hierarchy”**, for authority, subordination, and varying degrees of hierarchy.

### Subordinate state

**Definition.** The state whose security, alignment, or foreign-policy choices are substantially constrained by a dominant power.

**Main author/source.** No single originator; **David A. Lake** provides the closest general relational framework.

**Main related work(s).** Lake, *Hierarchy in International Relations* (2009), Chapters 1–2, **“International Hierarchy”** and **“Relational Contracting and International Hierarchy”**.

### Patron / client state

**Definition.** Terms for an asymmetric relationship in which institutionalized support and dependence are central. They should not automatically be treated as interchangeable with *dominant power* and *subordinate state*: patron–client analysis emphasizes a particular relationship of support, exchange, and dependence.

**Main author/source.** No single originator. **David A. Lake** is a related source for hierarchy, but his categories should not be presented as synonyms for every patron–client relationship.

**Main related work(s).** Lake, *Hierarchy in International Relations* (2009), Chapters 1–2, for the broader hierarchical setting. A dedicated patron–client literature should be cited if this distinction becomes central to the thesis.

### Hegemon / aligned state

**Definition.** A **hegemon** is a state powerful enough to dominate the other states in the relevant system or region. An **aligned state** is a state whose external orientation is coordinated with another power, without that coordination alone proving subordination.

**Main author/source.** **John J. Mearsheimer** for hegemon and regional hegemony; **Stephen M. Walt** for alliance alignment. *Aligned state* is a generic descriptive term.

**Main related work(s).** Mearsheimer, *The Tragedy of Great Power Politics* (2001), Chapter 2, **“Anarchy and the Struggle for Power”**, especially **“Hegemony’s Limits”**; Chapter 5, **“Strategies for Survival”**, for strategies used to gain and maintain power. Walt, *The Origins of Alliances* (1987), Chapters 1–2, **“Introduction: Exploring Alliance Formation”** and **“Explaining Alliance Formation”**, for alignment and alliance choices.

### Sphere of influence

**Definition.** A determinate region or relationship in which an external power exercises predominant influence, limiting the independence or freedom of action of political entities within it. This is more specific than *international relations* and does not necessarily imply complete control.

**Main author/source.** **Paul Keal** for the definition used here; **Susanna Hast** for the concept’s historical and theoretical construction.

**Main related work(s).** Keal, *Unspoken Rules and Superpower Dominance* (1983), **p. 15**, for predominant external influence and constrained independence. Hast, *Spheres of Influence in International Relations: History, Theory and Politics* (2014), **Introduction**, for the concept and its contemporary meanings, and Chapters 2–3 for its treatment in relation to the English School.

### External alignment

**Definition.** A state’s position vis-à-vis great powers, alliances, and spheres of influence. Entering or leaving a sphere of influence is one kind of change in external alignment, but not the only possible kind.

**Main author/source.** **Stephen M. Walt** for alliance formation and alignment; *external alignment* is used here as a broader framework label, not as Walt’s exclusive technical term.

**Main related work(s).** Walt, *The Origins of Alliances* (1987), Chapter 1, **“Introduction: Exploring Alliance Formation”**, and Chapter 2, **“Explaining Alliance Formation”**, especially the analysis of balancing and bandwagoning.

### Position within the international structure

**Definition.** A particular state’s location relative to other powers. A state changing its alignment undergoes a **positional change within the structure**; this does not, by itself, constitute a change in the international structure.

**Main author/source.** **Kenneth N. Waltz**, particularly his distinction between the arrangement of units and the distribution of capabilities across them.

**Main related work(s).** Waltz, *Theory of International Politics* (1979), Chapter 5, **“Political Structures”**, for structure and the placement of units; Chapter 6, **“Anarchic Orders and Balances of Power”**, for positions and changes in the distribution of capabilities.

## 4. Political and international phenomena

### Government turnover

**What changes.** The persons or parties holding governing office: presidents, ministers, or governing parties are replaced.

**Main author/source.** No single originator; this is an empirical event category. **Bueno de Mesquita and coauthors** provide the closest theory of leadership survival and replacement.

**Main related work(s).** Bueno de Mesquita et al., *The Logic of Political Survival* (2003), Chapters 1–2, **“The Logic of Political Survival”** and **“The Selectorate Theory”**.

### Ruling-coalition change

**What changes.** The wider set of actors whose support sustains rule, rather than merely the visible officeholders.

**Main author/source.** No single originator for this exact label. **Bueno de Mesquita and coauthors** are the principal related source for changes in the support coalition, subject to the distinction between a broad ruling coalition and their technical winning coalition.

**Main related work(s).** Bueno de Mesquita et al., *The Logic of Political Survival* (2003), Chapter 2, **“The Selectorate Theory”**.

### Regime change

**What changes.** The rules governing access to and organization of political power, rather than simply the identity of rulers.

**Main author/source.** **Juan J. Linz and Alfred Stepan** for regime types and transitions.

**Main related work(s).** Linz and Stepan, *Problems of Democratic Transition and Consolidation* (1996), Chapter 3, **“Modern Nondemocratic Regimes”**, and Chapter 4, **“The Implications of Prior Regime Type for Transition Paths and Consolidation Tasks”**.

### Transformation of the power structure

**What changes.** The effective concentration, separation, representation, or control of political power. This category makes it possible to distinguish nominal regime change from deeper structural continuity.

**Main author/source.** **Antonio García-Trevijano** is the principal theoretical anchor; the English event label is formulated for this framework.

**Main related work(s).** García-Trevijano, *Teoría pura de la república* (2010), **p. 81**, for the structure of political power; **p. 248**, for alternative structures of political organization; **pp. 369 and 374**, for control of power, representation, and separation of powers.

### State-apparatus restructuring

**What changes.** The durable administrative, coercive, judicial, or fiscal machinery of the state, rather than only the government or political regime.

**Main author/source.** **Theda Skocpol** for state transformation and state building; **Louis Althusser** for the distinction between state power and state apparatus.

**Main related work(s).** Skocpol, *States and Social Revolutions* (1979), Chapter 1, **“Explaining Social Revolutions: Alternatives to Existing Theories”**, and Chapter 4, **“What Changed and How: A Focus on State Building”**. Althusser, **“Ideology and Ideological State Apparatuses”**, section **“The State”**.

### Policy change

**What changes.** What the government does: its policy settings, instruments, or overarching goals. Policy change is not itself government turnover or regime change.

**Main author/source.** **Peter A. Hall** for different orders of policy change and policy-paradigm transformation.

**Main related work(s).** Hall, **“Policy Paradigms, Social Learning, and the State: The Case of Economic Policymaking in Britain”** (1993), **pp. 278–279**, for first-, second-, and third-order change.

### External realignment

**What changes.** A state’s alliance, patron, dominant-power relationship, or sphere of influence—in other words, its international position.

**Main author/source.** **Stephen M. Walt** for changes in alignment and alliance choice; the broader label is a framework convention.

**Main related work(s).** Walt, *The Origins of Alliances* (1987), Chapters 1–2, **“Introduction: Exploring Alliance Formation”** and **“Explaining Alliance Formation”**, particularly balancing and bandwagoning.

### Systemic power shift

**What changes.** The overall distribution of power or capabilities among states, rather than merely one state’s alignment within an unchanged structure.

**Main author/source.** **Robert Gilpin** for international political change and differential growth; **Kenneth N. Waltz** for distribution of capabilities as an element of structure.

**Main related work(s).** Gilpin, *War and Change in World Politics* (1981), Chapter 1, **“The Nature of International Political Change”**, and Chapter 2, **“Stability and Change”**. Waltz, *Theory of International Politics* (1979), Chapters 5–6.

**Two distinctions remain fundamental:**

> **Government turnover or even nominal regime change may occur without a transformation of the underlying structure of political power.**

> **A state’s external realignment may occur without a systemic power shift, although the two may coincide.**

## 5. Outcomes and conditioning conditions

### Economic disruption

**Definition.** The general economic phenomenon to be explained. It is preferable to *economic situation*, which is too static and broad. It may subsequently be differentiated into output collapse, inflation, capital flight, or another specific outcome.

**Main author/source.** No single originator; this is a framework-level dependent-outcome label. **Dani Rodrik** is a relevant related source for the narrower phenomenon of growth collapse following external shocks.

**Main related work(s).** Rodrik, **“Where Did All the Growth Go? External Shocks, Social Conflict, and Growth Collapses”** (1999), especially its analysis of external shocks and growth collapses. The article should not be treated as a definition of every form of economic disruption.

### Security position

**Definition.** The state’s strategic position relative to other states and threats. This should be kept analytically separate from economic outcomes.

**Main author/source.** **Kenneth N. Waltz** and **John J. Mearsheimer**, with different theoretical implications: Waltz emphasizes security under structural constraints, while Mearsheimer emphasizes power maximization and regional hegemony.

**Main related work(s).** Waltz, *Theory of International Politics* (1979), Chapter 6, **“Anarchic Orders and Balances of Power”**, and Chapter 8, **“Structural Causes and Military Effects”**. Mearsheimer, *The Tragedy of Great Power Politics* (2001), Chapter 2, **“Anarchy and the Struggle for Power”**, and Chapter 5, **“Strategies for Survival”**.

### Military capabilities

**Definition.** The military resources and capacities that a state can mobilize. They should not be combined with economic performance into a single, undifferentiated “economic and military situation.”

**Main author/source.** **John J. Mearsheimer**, particularly his distinction between latent power and actual military power.

**Main related work(s).** Mearsheimer, *The Tragedy of Great Power Politics* (2001), Chapter 3, **“Wealth and Power”**, for the relationship between potential and actual power, and Chapter 4, **“The Primacy of Land Power”**, for military forces and capabilities.

### Material endowments

**Definition.** Relatively durable conditions such as geography, natural resources, environment, and population that constrain economic and strategic possibilities.

**Main author/source.** No single originator for the broad category. **Jared Diamond** is a related source for geographical and environmental endowments; **Mearsheimer** is more directly relevant when population and wealth are treated as bases of latent power.

**Main related work(s).** Diamond, *Guns, Germs, and Steel* (1997), Prologue, **“Yali’s Question”**, Chapter 4, **“Farmer Power”**, and Chapter 5, **“History’s Haves and Have-Nots”**, for geographically uneven endowments. Mearsheimer, *The Tragedy of Great Power Politics* (2001), Chapter 3, **“Wealth and Power”**, for population and wealth.

### Material capabilities

**Definition.** Resources that a state can mobilize, especially economic and military capabilities. **Endowments** are conditions; **capabilities** are politically usable capacities.

**Main author/source.** **John J. Mearsheimer**, particularly for the conversion of population and wealth into latent and military power.

**Main related work(s).** Mearsheimer, *The Tragedy of Great Power Politics* (2001), Chapter 3, **“Wealth and Power”**, and Chapter 4, **“The Primacy of Land Power”**.

---

## Compact conceptual chain

> **States** are governed by incumbent **governments**, supported by **ruling coalitions**, operating within a **political regime** and an effective **structure of political power**, and acting through a durable **state apparatus**. Internationally, states possess a particular **external alignment** and **position within the international structure**, especially in relation to **great powers** and their **spheres of influence**. Their economic and military outcomes are conditioned by their **material endowments and capabilities**.

## Core distinction in one line

**Who rules — how power is organized — through what apparatus rule is exercised — where the state stands internationally — what outcomes follow.**
